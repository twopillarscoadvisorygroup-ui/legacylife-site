The 9 elements of the FTC Safeguards Rule, in plain English

By the Legacy Life team · November 16, 2026 · 7 min read

The FTC Safeguards Rule sets out the core pieces of an information security program for businesses the FTC treats as financial institutions, including tax preparers. The rule is written in legal language. Here’s what each of the nine elements means in everyday terms.

1. Put someone in charge

Designate a “Qualified Individual” to oversee your security program. In a small office this is often the owner. You can use an outside provider, but your business stays responsible.

2. Assess your risks in writing

Look at where customer information is stored and how it could be lost, stolen or misused. Write down what you find and how serious each risk is.

3. Put safeguards in place

Use protections that match your risks. The rule specifically calls for things like limiting who can access data, knowing what data and systems you have, encrypting customer information, using multi‑factor authentication, securely disposing of data you no longer need and keeping track of who does what on your systems.

4. Test and monitor

Regularly check that your safeguards are working, either with continuous monitoring or periodic testing such as vulnerability scans.

5. Train your people

Make sure staff know your security policies and how to follow them, with regular security awareness training.

6. Oversee your vendors

Choose service providers that can protect your data, require it in your agreements and check in on them periodically.

7. Keep the program current

Update your program when your business, technology or risks change, such as a new office, new software or a new remote employee.

8. Have an incident response plan

Write down what you’ll do if something goes wrong: who’s in charge, how you’ll contain the problem, who you need to notify and how you’ll fix the cause.

9. Report to leadership

Your Qualified Individual should report in writing, at least once a year, on the state of the program to the owner or board.

Good to know: the FTC also requires covered businesses to notify it after certain security events involving the information of 500 or more consumers. And some requirements are lighter for businesses that keep information on fewer than 5,000 consumers. Check the current rule or ask a professional how it applies to you.

Where a WISP fits

Your Written Information Security Plan is where you document all nine elements for your office. Done well, it becomes a practical guide your team can actually follow, not a binder that sits on a shelf.
